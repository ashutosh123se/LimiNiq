For over a decade, SEO strategy revolved around keywords and backlinks — match the query, earn the link, rank the page. That model still works, but it's no longer sufficient. Since the Knowledge Graph became central to how Google interprets queries, and even more so since AI Overviews and generative search results started summarizing answers directly, ranking well increasingly depends on something different: whether Google's systems recognize your business as a **verified entity** with a clear, consistent identity across the web.

This is entity SEO, and it's the framework we run for every LIMINIQ client regardless of industry.

## What an "entity" actually means to Google

An entity is a uniquely identifiable thing — a person, organization, place, or concept — that Google's Knowledge Graph can attach facts to and disambiguate from similarly named things. Google doesn't process your homepage as a string of keywords; it tries to resolve *what* and *who* is behind the page, then attaches confidence scores to facts about that entity: what industry you're in, where you're located, what you're known for, and who else vouches for you.

The practical implication: if Google can't confidently establish who you are, it will rank you cautiously even if your on-page content is technically excellent. Entity ambiguity is one of the most common reasons we see well-written content plateau just outside page one.

## The three pillars of entity authority

### 1. Structured data as a machine-readable identity card

Schema.org markup isn't a ranking hack — it's the clearest signal you can give Google about who you are. At minimum, every business site should implement:

- **Organization schema** with `name`, `logo`, `url`, `sameAs` (linking to verified social/business profiles), `address`, and `contactPoint`.
- **LocalBusiness schema** if you serve a physical or regional market, including `priceRange`, `areaServed`, and `aggregateRating` where genuinely earned.
- **Service** and **Article** schema on relevant pages, tying content back to the parent Organization entity via `@id` references so Google can connect the dots instead of guessing.

The `sameAs` property deserves special attention — it's the direct edge in the graph that says "this website and this LinkedIn/Crunchbase/Wikipedia entry are the same entity." Weak or missing `sameAs` links are the single most common gap we find in technical SEO audits.

### 2. Consistency across the entity's public footprint

Google cross-references your NAP (Name, Address, Phone) and brand description across every surface it can find: Google Business Profile, LinkedIn, Crunchbase, industry directories, press mentions, and your own site's footer. Inconsistency — a slightly different business name, an old address, a phone number that doesn't match — introduces doubt into the entity resolution process and dilutes confidence scores.

Our audit checklist covers:

- Exact NAP match across Google Business Profile, footer schema, and any directory listings.
- A consistent one-sentence business description used verbatim (or near-verbatim) across LinkedIn, Crunchbase, and your About page.
- Author bylines on content that map to real, describable people with their own minimal entity footprint (a LinkedIn profile, a consistent name spelling, an author bio linked from every article they write).

### 3. Topical authority through content clustering

Entities aren't just companies — they're also topics and concepts. If you want to be recognized as an authority on "SaaS MVP development," a single well-optimized page isn't enough. Google looks for a *cluster*: multiple interlinked pieces of content that comprehensively cover the topic's subtopics, cross-linked to each other and to a central pillar page.

The structure we build for clients:

1. A pillar page targeting the broad topic (e.g., "SaaS MVP Development").
2. Supporting cluster content targeting specific subtopics (pricing models, tech stack choices, common failure modes, timeline benchmarks).
3. Internal links from every cluster page back to the pillar, and cross-links between related cluster pages — this is what signals topical depth, not just keyword coverage.

## Why this matters more with AI Overviews and generative search

When Google (or Perplexity, or ChatGPT search) generates a synthesized answer instead of a list of blue links, it's pulling from sources it trusts to represent a topic accurately. Entity-ambiguous sites — sites where the underlying business identity isn't clearly established — are far less likely to be cited as a source in generative answers, because there's no confident attribution path back to a real, verifiable entity.

This is a structural shift, not a temporary algorithm update. Sites that invest in entity clarity now are building a moat that compounds as more search behavior shifts toward AI-mediated answers.

## A practical 90-day entity SEO rollout

For clients starting from zero, we sequence the work like this:

**Weeks 1–2: Foundation audit.** Map every existing mention of the business across the web, identify NAP inconsistencies, and audit current schema implementation (most sites have partial or broken JSON-LD).

**Weeks 3–4: Structured data implementation.** Roll out Organization, LocalBusiness, and Article schema with correct `@id` linking, verified against Google's Rich Results Test and Schema Markup Validator.

**Weeks 5–8: Footprint consolidation.** Claim and standardize Google Business Profile, LinkedIn company page, Crunchbase, and relevant industry directories with identical NAP and description.

**Weeks 9–12: Topical cluster build-out.** Launch or restructure pillar and cluster content around the two or three topics the business most wants to own, with full internal linking.

## The compounding effect

Entity SEO doesn't produce the fast, volatile wins that a single viral backlink might. It produces something more valuable: a durable identity that search engines (and increasingly, AI models) trust enough to cite, recommend, and rank consistently — even as algorithms change around it. Every client we've run this framework for has seen the same pattern: slower initial movement, followed by rankings that are far more resistant to future core updates than keyword-only optimization ever was.
